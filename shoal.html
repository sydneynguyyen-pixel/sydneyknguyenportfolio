Context

Caring for someone who is dying is hard. Coordinating it alone is harder.

When someone is dying at home, one person usually becomes the coordinator: prescriptions, hospice visits, insurance calls. The tasks live in group texts and one person's head, and no one else can see who is doing what. I wanted to find a way for a family to split the work without one person having to keep asking for help.

  • 63 millionpeople in the U.S. are family caregivers. That's nearly 1 in 4 adults.AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025
  • 61 hoursof help a week goes to an older adult in their last year of life, split across 2.5 caregivers on average.Ornstein et al., Health Affairs, 2017
  • 1 in 4family caregivers say they feel socially isolated.AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025

A jar for each person, a stone for each task.

Shoal is an app for sharing care tasks, with an optional set of jars and light-up stones for the kitchen counter. Each stone is a task and each jar is a person. The kit needs the app to work, but the app works without the kit. We added the kit so a family can see who has what at a glance, without opening a phone or reading through a long list of tasks. To take on a task, you put its stone in your jar. To pass a task on, you ask someone, and it only moves if they say yes. Shoal helps a family organize care. It does not give medical advice.

We talked to five people, twice each.

Three family caregivers who had lost a parent or grandparent, a hospice night nurse, and an end-of-life doula (someone who supports people and families through dying). The first time, we learned what they struggled with. The second time, we showed them early designs. Names are changed.

Three moments that mattered.

From three of the interviews, we picked one moment each and broke it into five steps. The dark dots mark the hardest steps.

A thought June was ashamed of
June · family caregiver
  1. Intention
  2. Beginning
  3. Middle
  4. Unexpected
  5. Ending
What happened
June was driving alone to a grandparent's house for another caregiving shift, months into putting their own life on hold. A thought came up: "I wish something would happen so I can't help." June felt instant shame, and anger at a sibling who wasn't helping. They kept driving, arrived, and carried on without telling anyone.
How they felt
Exhausted and isolated, then ashamed and angry, then numb.
Opportunity
A private place to say a thought like that out loud, and check-ins for the caregiver, not just the patient.
Why it matters
When one person does all the work, they also handle the stress alone. That is the problem we wanted to solve.
The unsigned documents
Wren · end-of-life doula
  1. Intention
  2. Beginning
  3. Middle
  4. Unexpected
  5. Ending
What happened
Wren had a client with no family or friends and little time left. They did everything right: a will, a Five Wishes form (a kind of living will), hours of recorded wishes, and a lawyer. But the client kept putting off the final signature, was admitted to hospital, and died suddenly. Because the documents were never signed or witnessed, they didn't count legally. Wren went to the hospital and told the medical team the wishes out loud.
How they felt
Focused and organised, then quietly uneasy, then overwhelmed and confused. At the end, calm.
Opportunity
One place the doula and lawyer can both check, so nothing is missed, and a way to witness documents for people who have no one to ask.
Why it matters
Even the professionals couldn't see who had done what. Poor coordination isn't only a family problem. It happens between professionals too.
Taking breaks in pairs
Sana · family caregiver
  1. Intention
  2. Beginning
  3. Middle
  4. Unexpected
  5. Ending
What happened
Sana's family was at a hospital bedside, overwhelmed, and needed a break without leaving. Without planning it, they started taking breaks in pairs, like going for lunch or a smoke. Sana realised a sibling was hiding how they were really doing.
How they felt
Tense, then comforted and connected, then concerned, then relieved.
Opportunity
A way to say "I need a minute" without explaining, and a prompt to check in on the quietest person.
Why it matters
Families already look out for each other, but they don't always notice who is struggling. We wanted to make that easier to see.

Define

We wrote five questions to design around, and chose one to focus on.

We rewrote each problem from our research as a question starting with "How might we…". Michelle and I wrote five, then chose "Share the load" to design for, because it's about the one family member who ends up organizing everyone's care.

Design criteria

Taking work off people's plates ranked first.

Once we had a rough version, we wrote five goals for the design based on the interviews, and asked all five people to rank them. Their ranking decided what the brand and the app focused on.

Testing

What we learned from testing

We tested with the same five people. First we showed 3D images of the kit with no explanation, to see what people thought it was. Then we walked them through the stones, jars, rings and app. Use the arrows to see what they said.

01 Glow colors

Glow colors

What we showed

Stones glowed to show a task's status: no glow, blue when someone is doing it, yellow when it's due soon.

What we heard
"White and blue are too similar."

Some family members are colorblind. One person suggested red for urgent and green for owned.

What we changed

People can now change the glow colors to whatever works for them.

02 The rings

The rings

What we showed

A ring under each jar shows how busy that person is.

What we heard
"[The rings] could make the main caregiver feel worse."

One person said rings weren't relevant to them.

What we changed

We took the rings off the jars.

03 The jars

The jars

What we showed

Clear jars of stones, side by side on the kitchen counter.

What we heard
"A high-end Post-it note."

One person found the jars stressful and "a lot of stuff", and liked the app much more.

04 Sharing with a family code

Sharing with a family code

What we showed

A code a family can share, so friends and neighbours can help with a task without making an account.

What we heard
"I don't trust anyone I don't know."

People worried about privacy. One asked whether a postponed task could get a family reported for neglect.

What we changed

Shoal is private by default, and the family decides who sees what.

05 Medication tracking

Medication tracking

What we didn't show

It was not in our designs yet.

What we heard

The hospice nurse suggested a shared log of who gave which dose and when, after seeing double and missed doses of morphine at home.

What we changed

We added a shared medicine log, so everyone can see when a dose was already given.

Final Product

Each jar is a person. Each stone is a task.

Every person in the family has a jar on the counter. You pair a stone to a task in the app, then drop it in the jar of whoever is doing it. Its light shows where the task stands: white when no one has it yet, blue when someone is on it, yellow when it's due soon, and off when it's done. The stones show no task details on purpose, so the counter doesn't add stress. The details stay in the app.

I mocked the screens in Figma, then built a clickable prototype in code. Pebble, the app's AI assistant, runs on a few simple rules in the prototype, so there is no real AI model behind it yet.

Pairing a stone, in the app and in the kit

Feature highlight 1

See who has room at a glance.

Home shows every person's jar side by side. A fuller jar means more tasks, so you can see who is busy and who has time without opening anything. Below it, the week view shows which day has the most tasks.

Home, and the week view

Feature highlight 2

Handing a task off.

My jar asks how your load feels, and only you see the answer. If you say it is a lot, Pebble looks for someone with room. You choose who gets the request: one person, a few, or everyone. The first person to say yes takes the task.

Handing a task to Mom

Feature highlight 3

Outside help.

Families can bring in help from outside at two levels. The trusted circle is for people they know well. The volunteer code is for anyone they give a 4-digit code to. Volunteers help from afar, with tasks like ordering from the family's wish list. When a volunteer marks a task done, the family gets a notification and can send them a thank-you card. Families can post the card on a thanks wall that volunteers see, without their own names.

Reflection

What we learned, and what's next.

What we learned about interviewing.

Hospice is a really hard and sensitive topic to talk about, so we learned a lot about interviewing: how to approach sensitive topics, how to cover the emotional, technical and physical sides of the design, and how to use HCI scales and ratings.

Designing the app and the kit together.

Every choice in one affected the other. The app had to work for families who don't have the kit, and the kit could only show what the app knew. We kept task details in the app and gave each stone only a color, so the counter shows who has what without adding stress.

Designing for trust and privacy.

These families are sharing private things at a hard time. When we tested sharing with a family code, people told us "I don't trust anyone I don't know," and one asked whether a postponed task could get a family reported for neglect. So Shoal is private by default, and the family decides who sees what.

Where we'd take Shoal next.

Test with a family in their own home · build a real stone that connects to the app · ask more about the physical work of caregiving · decide how Pebble should behave, like who approves its summaries and whether it should go quiet in the last days · design for the person who is dying, and for what happens after they die.
