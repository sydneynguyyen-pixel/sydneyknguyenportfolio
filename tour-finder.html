Context

On a tour, the jobs are filled before the tour is announced.

In live music, the people who run a tour are usually hired through personal networks. The work goes to whoever notices first that an artist is about to tour, and reaches out.

The idea came from someone close to me.

They coordinate tours and production, and found work by researching artists by hand: guessing who might tour soon, checking whether the artist already had a team, and cold-emailing when they saw an opening. It worked, but only for the artists who came to mind, and it didn't scale.

Tour Finder looks for artists who are likely to tour soon.

Once a tour is announced, someone has usually already been hired to run it. Tour Finder is a discovery tool, not a job board. It looks for signs that an artist will tour soon, like a new release with no tour booked yet, a return after time away, or more frequent shows. Then it ranks those artists by how well they fit you, while there is still time to reach out.

Who it's for

Built first for independent tour crew.

Tour managers and production crew who work from one gig to the next live with this problem every day. The same early signal could also help anyone who sells to tours.

Building it

One weekly pipeline, with no database.

I built Tour Finder alone, working with Claude Code. It went from an empty repo to a live, running pipeline in about two days. Every week, a job pulls artist data from free public sources, scores the artists, and saves the results to the project's GitHub repo. The dashboard reads those results.

How the data moves
  • Release infoDeezer, MusicBrainz, TheAudioDB, Wikidata
  • Tour historySetlist.fm, Last.fm
  • News and checksDiscogs, RSS feeds
  • On-sale datesTicketmaster, JamBase
  1. Automation pipelineRuns every week on GitHub Actions
  2. GitHub repoStores the results. No database needed.
  3. DashboardReact, live on Netlify

Edits made in the dashboard sync back to the repo through a Netlify function.

Four decisions shaped the build.

Free, public data

No paid API. Setlist.fm, MusicBrainz, TheAudioDB, Deezer, Wikidata, Last.fm, Discogs and RSS feeds give release, tour and genre data. Ticketmaster and JamBase give confirmed on-sale dates and venues.

I first planned the project around a major streaming platform's API. Access to it was restricted partway through the build, so I moved to these sources.

Finding new artists

Instead of a fixed watchlist, it starts from the artists you've worked with and follows an artist-similarity graph to similar artists you haven't worked with. It leaves out artists you already know and scores the rest, so the results get more personal as you log more artists.

Scoring

A weighted score across five factors. Timing counts the most: a new release with no tour booked yet is the strongest signal.

It also tells a real comeback apart from a false signal, like a remix released after years of no activity, by weighting original music higher.

GitHub as the backend

The weekly job saves its results straight to the repo. The dashboard is a static React app that reads them from GitHub, so there is no server to keep running.

Final Product

One dashboard, checked once a week.

Each week, Tour Finder brings in new leads. You can see who each artist is, the artists you've worked with shape the next round, and you can remove the ones that aren't a fit.

Feature highlight 1

A score from 0 to 100 for each artist.

The score shows who is most worth reaching out to. It comes from five factors: touring history, how well the artist's venue size matches yours, how easy their team is to reach, timing, and momentum.

An artist's score, its breakdown, and the plain-language explanation

Feature highlight 2

Everything you'd research about an artist, in one place.

Each artist has a page with their tour history, recent releases, contact info, and a plain-language explanation of why they got their score.

Switching between the three tabs on an artist's page

Feature highlight 3

The artists you've worked with shape who it suggests next.

My Artists is a list of the artists you've actually worked with, kept separate from the suggested leads. The scoring also learns from it.

My Artists

Feature highlight 4

Tours that just went on sale.

A feed of confirmed tours that are on sale but haven't started yet, soonest first, pulled from ticketing data. Artists you track show up right away. Anyone else needs a real multi-city run to show up.

The New Tour Detected feed, sorted soonest first, then furthest out first

Other features.

Rank your genres

Genres are ranked into tiers. Artists in genres near the top get a small scoring boost, and genres near the bottom get a slight reduction. Nothing is left out. In the live tool, you drag genres to reorder them.

Scan any time

New leads come in automatically every week. In the live tool, you can also start a scan yourself and watch the results come in.

Reflection

What I learned, and what's next.

Building it showed the problem is real.

In this industry, the chance to get hired depends on reaching out at the right time, and the weekly scan shows that every week. Tour Finder is live and used by one person for now. The scan runs on schedule and adds to a growing list of tracked artists.

The hardest part was keeping edits in sync.

Early on, coming back to the dashboard could overwrite newer data with an older copy saved in the browser. I fixed it by comparing the browser's copy with the backend on every visit, instead of trusting whichever one saved last.

What's next.

Save genre preferences the same way artist logs are already saved · adjust the scoring weights as more real leads come in · open it up to more people, with accounts and sign-in · look into paid and business data sources for data I can't get today.
